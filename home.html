find your way to value

We will start our engagement with you with one question:

What do you value, as an organization?

Your answer will determine the nature of our relationship.

And whatever your answer, you have almost certainly encountered difficulties and obstacles, taken wrong turns, and tasted poisonous fruit along the way, in your pursuit of value. Perhaps that's the case right now, and if so, it is a good time to look into the symptoms and causes of your pain.

01

Symptoms and Causes

Healthier revenue streams, higher customer and employee satisfaction, and sustainable growth are all results and benefits of continuous change in behaviour of an organization and its people. Change in behaviour requires change in knowledge, and change in knowledge requires the experience of taking action, and witnessing, observing and thinking about the outcome.

This experience, processed into knowledge, enables us to make judgements and decisions that will surface as behaviours, which will, in turn, direct the organization's path towards value.

Now, what you will most often approach us with, or what we will most often identify in your organizational activity is a symptom: a concrete problem or dysfunction that is hurting you and your efforts to create value for yourselves and your market.

You might want us to deal with surface-level, market-facing symptoms that are hurting your revenue, profit margins and bottom line, such as:

  • marketing that gets plenty of attention, but not from the people who would enter a value exchange with you,
  • lead generation that fills the pipeline with names, and the calendar with conversations that were never going to go anywhere,
  • sales workflows where the right prospects stall, go quiet, and get written off as bad timing,
  • customer care that hears the complaints, but never the reasons people stay or leave.

Or you might approach us with deeper-lying symptoms that are affecting internal and external communication flows, employee motivation, or strategic and operational decision making.

What we know from experience is that these layers of symptoms are, in fact, intertwined and interdependent. Whichever symptom we address first will almost certainly lead us to deeper underlying causes, which are in turn symptoms of even deeper causes. Like a ball of string, unraveling in the Minotaur's labyrinth, we will plot our way through the corridors until we hit the light of day.

This means we can deal with one symptom at a time, along with the assumptions about what is causing it. Which brings us to our way of work.

02 · The framework

Way of Work

The Way of Work turns one symptom, and the assumptions we hold about what is causing it, into one improvement that holds. It runs in four tracks, Discovery, Delivery, Validation and Integration, which are not phases that follow one another, but run continuously and in parallel, for as long as we work together.

Each cycle starts with what you know and what you are assuming, builds it into an interface that carries your value to the audience it is meant for, and reads what comes back. What we learn there folds into the way your organization operates, as a value increment: what worked is kept and built on, what did not is left behind. That is one turn of the thread, and then we do it again.

Values

Four commitments that hold in every cycle, whichever instrument we happen to have in our hands.

Before we take the first step in addressing the identified symptom and its causes, we need to get our bearings straight, in terms of who needs to be involved and what their roles are, for each cycle in the way of work. For this we need a compass.

05 · The framework

The Compass

The Compass is a 360 degree map of your stakeholders. It has four points: the market you want to attract, the customers you already serve, the employees who carry your values into the world every day, and the partners and vendors you depend on to deliver. A symptom rarely sits in just one of these conversations. It will usually show up in several at once, and sometimes in all four, which is why we map where it spreads across the whole circle before we decide anything.

That map is one half of the work. The other half is planning what the cycle will take: which of the four instruments it will draw on, who inside your organization needs to be in the room, and what the work will ask of them. Seeing where a symptom spreads is also what lets us choose where to enter it, because we still take one conversation at a time. The people closest to that conversation are the ones holding the knowledge and the assumptions we are about to test.
